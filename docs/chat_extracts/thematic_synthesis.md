''In the fast-paced world of Agile methodology, the ability to iterate quickly and deliver features rapidly is celebrated. However, this speed often comes at a significant cost: a fragmented approach to design. Agile’s piecemeal nature, while efficient in delivering incremental improvements, can create a web of inconsistencies in user flows, visual design, and overall user experiences.

This article delves into how Agile’s inherent fragmentation can lead to disconnects, gaps, and disruptions in a project’s design process, all while highlighting the challenge of maintaining a coherent, holistic perspective across a product’s development.

The Nature of Agile: A Double-Edged Sword
Agile is built on short, iterative cycles, often known as sprints, designed to deliver small pieces of functionality at a rapid pace. These rapid iterations are praised for responding to evolving user needs and market demands, but they can sometimes sacrifice long-term vision in the name of immediate outputs.

In a design context, this leads to several risks. Without a consistent and deliberate design approach, teams can fall into the trap of focusing too much on individual tasks or isolated features, resulting in disjointed design outcomes that don’t integrate well with one another. Let’s explore the specific issues Agile’s fragmented approach can introduce into design.

Inconsistent User Flows
One of the most immediate consequences of Agile’s fragmented approach is inconsistent user flows. A user flow is a step-by-step guide that leads a user through a product or service, ensuring they can accomplish tasks smoothly and with minimal confusion. Agile teams, especially when design decisions are made in isolation or without proper collaboration, often find themselves creating flows that don’t mesh together.

For instance, different teams may work on different user tasks in various sprints — resulting in disjointed navigation, unclear transitions, and awkward, confusing sequences that hinder the overall user experience. While each individual flow may seem functional on its own, when combined, the product often feels disconnected, forcing users to relearn how to interact with different parts of the application or website.

Visual Inconsistencies
Visual consistency is another area where Agile’s fragmented approach can lead to problems. As designers work on different parts of the product during each sprint, it can become challenging to maintain uniformity in visual elements like colors, typography, buttons, and layout styles. Agile’s emphasis on speed often makes it inevitable to inspect for designers.

What this creates is an interface where visual elements are treated as secondary to functionality, leading to disjointed aesthetics. A user moving through a product may experience sudden shifts in color palettes, typography, or button styles — disturbing the visual harmony. Visual inconsistencies disrupt the flow of engagement and could even result in users perceiving the product as unprofessional or unpolished.

Disconnected User Journeys
While user flows focus on task-specific navigation, user journeys take a more holistic approach by mapping out the entire experience a user has with a product or service. The trouble with Agile’s fragmented approach is that it rarely addresses the broader experience over time. Sprints focus on isolated features or user tasks, often ignoring how a user might journey from start to finish across the entire platform. As a result, the entire experience can feel disjointed.

For example, when working in Agile, a team may address only a specific interaction flow — say, the sign-up process — without considering how that fits into the user’s experience once they’re logged in. The onboarding experience may not be aligned with other interactions, such as subsequent engagements or how the user will return after an extended period of time. This disjointedness can leave users frustrated as they struggle to make sense of the fragmented journey, hampering their overall experience.

Technical Gaps
In addition to design inconsistencies, technical gaps arise when Agile teams develop features in isolation from one another without a broad understanding of the entire system. Since developers focus on specific features during each sprint, integration between components can be overlooked or handled ineffectively. Features may be delivered on time, but when they are combined, compatibility and functionality issues may arise, such as inconsistent database calls, poor API integration, or usability issues stemming from inefficient back-end development.

When technical decisions are made separately without comprehensive forethought, it becomes much harder to maintain a seamless user experience. These gaps in the system create an experience that is unreliable or broken at times, which leads to user frustration and mistrust in the product.

Perhaps the biggest challenge in Agile is ensuring that design and development teams maintain a holistic perspective while addressing the minutiae of individual features. Agile teams typically have different designers, developers, and product owners working on separate elements of a product, each focusing on their tasks within specific sprints. But a product that lacks an overarching vision or design direction risks becoming a patchwork of disconnected ideas.

To counteract this, it’s vital for designers and developers to stay aligned by embracing collaborative working methods that allow everyone to regularly update and inform one another about ongoing progress. However, this can be difficult to achieve within the constraints of a fast-paced Agile environment, particularly if communication channels aren’t fully optimized or if design teams don’t have the time or resources to revisit earlier work.

The fragmented nature of Agile design can create challenges when it comes to maintaining consistency and cohesion across user experiences, from user flows to visual identity and the overarching journey. Although Agile can be efficient in driving rapid innovation and delivering features quickly, design teams must recognize the risks of disconnection, inconsistency, and fragmentation that come with such a method. Maintaining a holistic approach by focusing on collaboration, establishing a solid design system, and keeping users central to the process can help bridge these gaps and enable Agile teams to produce polished, cohesive, and user-centric products.''

“Any organization that designs a system (defined broadly) will produce a design whose structure is a copy of the organization’s communication structure.”

Melvin Conway, 1968

In over half a century, Conway’s Law has aged gracefully — if anything, growing sharper and more revealing as digital systems, teams, and tools have proliferated. The irony? While Agile, Lean, and DevOps have transformed how we deliver software, the communication patterns and structural incentives inside many organizations remain rigid — and invisible. And nowhere is this more evident than in how UX design is silently, steadily, and systematically compromised.

Let me explain.

Backend-First Cultures: Where Structure Becomes Product
In many product teams, especially in traditional or enterprise-driven organizations, the back-end architecture isn’t just the technical foundation; It becomes the de facto product strategy.

This isn’t just a technical constraint. It’s an organizational mirror.

Teams are formed around back-end services. Responsibilities are carved along the lines of APIs, not user journeys. Prioritization is tied to database migration timelines or legacy infrastructure needs. And inevitably, the design process — ideally user-first — is reverse-engineered to fit the backend’s existing contracts, limitations, or sequencing.

The result? Users don’t experience flows. They experience fragments.

What does this look like on the ground?
Design decisions are made after the tech stack is finalized.
Flow compromises are made to fit the structure of legacy APIs.
PMs say: “This flow is too expensive to change right now — let’s go with what we have.”
Designers are asked to “just design around the back-end.”
And all of it seems logical. After all, there are real constraints: time, budget, sprint velocity, team bandwidth.

But Conway would remind us:

“If we believe our homomorphism, then we must agree that it does. To the extent that an organization is not completely flexible in its communication structure, that organization will stamp out an image of itself in every design it produces. The larger an organization is, the less flexibility it has and the more pronounced is the phenomenon.”

Budget, Sprints, and the Myth of Incremental UX
Agile has taught teams to work small, think fast, and move iteratively. But in practice, it often becomes a license to ignore system-level UX coherence.

You’ve likely heard these lines:

“Let’s just release it and see.”
“We’ll fix UX in the next sprint.”
“There’s no time to revisit the onboarding — let’s optimize the dashboard first.”
The problem isn’t urgency. It’s orientation.

Agile, as practiced in many organizations, tends to decompose product development into atomized tickets. Those tickets reflect the team’s internal structure, not the user’s mental model. When each story is implemented to match a specific service, team, or sprint commitment, UX becomes fragmented not because of incompetence, but because of structure.

Conway again:

“Given any design team organization, there is a class of design alternatives which cannot be effectively pursued by such an organization because the necessary communication paths do not exist.”

The Anatomy of Compromise
The designer sees the disjointedness. They’ve mapped the ideal flow.
The research is clear.
The friction is known.

But when they raise the issue, they hear:

“This would require rewriting the service.”
“The front-end can’t talk to that database yet.”
“We already committed the sprint.”
And so compromise is made.
Not once, but repeatedly.
And each micro-compromise calcifies into the system’s permanent shape.

Over time:

Workarounds replace holistic thinking.
UX debt accumulates.
Design systems fragment.
Users get confused — but no one remembers how it happened.
Because it didn’t happen all at once.
It happened organizationally.

So What Does Conway See?
From Mel Conway’s perspective, this isn’t a UX problem. It’s not even a dev problem.
It’s a reflection problem.

Your product isn’t broken because of code.
It’s broken because of how your teams are wired to talk — and how that shapes what gets prioritized, compromised, and eventually shipped.

This means:

If your architecture leads design, your UX will always lag.
If your structure fragments conversation, your product will fragment too.
If no one is empowered to challenge technical-first decisions, the user will always be second.
The Systems We Deserve
When organizations repeatedly compromise UX under the name of budget, sprint planning, or technical limitation, they aren’t being lazy. They’re being consistent with their structure.

The tragedy isn’t the compromise.
The tragedy is that the compromise becomes normalized.
That “designing around the backend” becomes how things are done.
That scalable, intentional, and strategic design becomes an edge case.

But again, Conway would say: that’s just how systems work. They reflect us.

And unless we change how we think, speak, and structure ourselves — we’ll keep designing systems that work beautifully for the org chart, but poorly for the human on the other side of the screen.

03.03.2026 - v4 - The Myth of B2C-ification: Why Consumer-Grade UX Fails Enterprise Complexity
For the past decade, a single directive has quietly dominated countless product roadmaps: Let's make it more like a consumer app. It sounds progressive and signals a genuine care for the user. We've grown accustomed to booking travel in seconds, transferring money instantly, and navigating ecosystems like Apple and Airbnb without ever reading a manual. Naturally, we want that same frictionless refinement in our enterprise tools.
But after years of designing in regulated environments - particularly in insurance platforms, I've come to a different conclusion. Enterprise software is not just a consumer product waiting to be simplified; it is a structurally different organism. When we try to "B2C-ify" it without respecting its underlying architecture, we don't actually eliminate complexity. We simply hide it somewhere less visible.
The Seduction of Visual Simplicity
Consumer products have undeniably raised the bar. Tools like Slack proved that business software didn't have to feel hostile, establishing visual clarity and responsiveness as baseline expectations. But over time, visual polish started being equated with operational simplicity.
A consumer booking flow works seamlessly because the decision space is narrow and the risk surface is limited. Compare that to an insurance underwriting platform. Behind a single policy issuance, you are likely dealing with:
Dynamic pricing models
Regulatory constraints
Risk-scoring algorithms
Channel-specific commission structures
Multi-layer approval chains
Strict audit requirements

That complexity isn't the result of poor design. It reflects legal obligations, financial exposure, and institutional accountability. The real challenge in enterprise UX isn't to erase this complexity, but to structure it in a way that makes it understandable and manageable.
Permission is Architecture, Not a Feature
In consumer apps, access is mostly binary: you're either logged in or you aren't. In enterprise systems, permissions define reality. Every screen behaves differently depending on the user's role, and every action carries strict boundaries:
An agent may be able to quote a price, but not override the underwriting logic.
A branch manager may approve discounts, but only within specific limits.
A compliance officer must be able to audit records, but restricted from editing them.
A partner bank may see distribution data, but not the core actuarial assumptions.

You aren't just designing pages; you're designing a controlled environment governed by policy. Minimalism is helpful, but clarity about authority and consequence matters much more.
Workflows Are Specialized and High-Stakes
Enterprise systems are rarely optimized for casual engagement. They are built for accuracy, throughput, compliance, and risk mitigation.
I once worked on an underwriting tool where a specific field looked completely redundant during a UI simplification exercise. It turned out to be the only visible trigger for a downstream approval workflow. Removing it didn't simplify the system, it obscured how the system actually functioned.
This is the subtle danger of surface-level reduction. Consider a digital retirement (BES) sales flow integrated with a bank's distribution channel. A single step can trigger regulatory disclosures, eligibility validations, accounting implications, and commission calculations. If an interface hides too much of that logic in the name of a clean UI, users inevitably compensate. They export data. They build shadow spreadsheets. They create parallel offline processes. The UI might look cleaner, but the organization quietly becomes more fragmented.
Decision Density Is Higher
Enterprise environments operate under intense cognitive and financial loads. Consider the daily realities of these roles:
A pricing manager reviewing loss ratios.
An operations lead monitoring a policy issuance backlog.
A fraud analyst evaluating anomaly signals.

These professionals depend on constructed visibility. Reducing visual density without understanding their "decision density" strips away the context they rely on to do their jobs safely. A clean screen isn't inherently better; a clear and trustworthy one is.
The Gravity of Product Culture
There is a subtle dynamic that reinforces the myth of B2C-ification. In fast-moving product organizations, especially under delivery pressure, conversations naturally gravitate toward what is visible. Screens are easy to demo, they fit neatly into sprints, and they feel concrete.
Discussions often begin with reasonable questions: Can we simplify this page? Do we really need all these fields? But in regulated systems, the core complexity rarely lives on the visible layer. It sits in business rules, decision trees, and interdependent workflows. When we debate UI before examining the underlying logic, we end up rearranging complexity rather than resolving it.
Over time, I've learned that the most valuable contribution design can make in these environments isn't stylistic refinement. It's structural modeling. Mapping decision flows, clarifying permission topologies, and identifying where friction is actually necessary for safety.
From Polished Screens to Living Systems
This requires a fundamental shift in how we think about components. Too often, they are treated as static rectangles drawn in a design tool. But in a regulated platform, components are behavioral units inside a larger system.
A data table in an underwriting environment is not just a container for rows. It has to carry:
Role-based visibility
Editable and read-only states
Inline validation logic
Audit trace implications
Bulk operation constraints
Exception handling behavior

It isn't just a rectangle; it's a policy-aware object operating within a regulatory ecosystem. When teams start treating components as evolving capabilities with defined states and rules, the conversation becomes systemic. A rigid interface will always struggle under the pressure of changing laws and operational restructuring, but a well-structured system adapts.
What We Should Actually Borrow from B2C
This is not a rejection of consumer UX principles. Enterprise software absolutely needs clear hierarchy, thoughtful error messaging, accessibility rigor, and human-centered onboarding. But those are universal qualities of good software, not assumptions about simplicity.
Consumer products optimize for individual adoption. Enterprise systems optimize for organizational alignment and throughput. Those are entirely different objectives.
Instead of asking, "How do we make this feel like a consumer app?" we should be asking:
Where must complexity remain visible for safety?
Where can it be progressively disclosed?
Which users require power, and which require guidance?
What is the cost of an error at each step?

Scalability Is Cultural Before It Is Technical
In regulated industries, long-term scalability is not achieved through cleaner screens alone. It emerges from organizational maturity. The ability to model systems clearly, document behaviors rigorously, and align product decisions with institutional logic.
The myth of B2C-ification is appealing because it promises simplicity through aesthetics. But true structural integrity isn't a visual upgrade. It's a cultural one. When a product culture shifts from merely shaping interfaces to engineering living systems, complexity finally becomes navigable rather than suppressed.

''In the fast-paced world of Agile methodology, the ability to iterate quickly and deliver features rapidly is celebrated. However, this speed often comes at a significant cost: a fragmented approach to design. Agile’s piecemeal nature, while efficient in delivering incremental improvements, can create a web of inconsistencies in user flows, visual design, and overall user experiences.

This article delves into how Agile’s inherent fragmentation can lead to disconnects, gaps, and disruptions in a project’s design process, all while highlighting the challenge of maintaining a coherent, holistic perspective across a product’s development.

The Nature of Agile: A Double-Edged Sword
Agile is built on short, iterative cycles, often known as sprints, designed to deliver small pieces of functionality at a rapid pace. These rapid iterations are praised for responding to evolving user needs and market demands, but they can sometimes sacrifice long-term vision in the name of immediate outputs.

In a design context, this leads to several risks. Without a consistent and deliberate design approach, teams can fall into the trap of focusing too much on individual tasks or isolated features, resulting in disjointed design outcomes that don’t integrate well with one another. Let’s explore the specific issues Agile’s fragmented approach can introduce into design.

Inconsistent User Flows
One of the most immediate consequences of Agile’s fragmented approach is inconsistent user flows. A user flow is a step-by-step guide that leads a user through a product or service, ensuring they can accomplish tasks smoothly and with minimal confusion. Agile teams, especially when design decisions are made in isolation or without proper collaboration, often find themselves creating flows that don’t mesh together.

For instance, different teams may work on different user tasks in various sprints — resulting in disjointed navigation, unclear transitions, and awkward, confusing sequences that hinder the overall user experience. While each individual flow may seem functional on its own, when combined, the product often feels disconnected, forcing users to relearn how to interact with different parts of the application or website.

Visual Inconsistencies
Visual consistency is another area where Agile’s fragmented approach can lead to problems. As designers work on different parts of the product during each sprint, it can become challenging to maintain uniformity in visual elements like colors, typography, buttons, and layout styles. Agile’s emphasis on speed often makes it inevitable to inspect for designers.

What this creates is an interface where visual elements are treated as secondary to functionality, leading to disjointed aesthetics. A user moving through a product may experience sudden shifts in color palettes, typography, or button styles — disturbing the visual harmony. Visual inconsistencies disrupt the flow of engagement and could even result in users perceiving the product as unprofessional or unpolished.

Disconnected User Journeys
While user flows focus on task-specific navigation, user journeys take a more holistic approach by mapping out the entire experience a user has with a product or service. The trouble with Agile’s fragmented approach is that it rarely addresses the broader experience over time. Sprints focus on isolated features or user tasks, often ignoring how a user might journey from start to finish across the entire platform. As a result, the entire experience can feel disjointed.

For example, when working in Agile, a team may address only a specific interaction flow — say, the sign-up process — without considering how that fits into the user’s experience once they’re logged in. The onboarding experience may not be aligned with other interactions, such as subsequent engagements or how the user will return after an extended period of time. This disjointedness can leave users frustrated as they struggle to make sense of the fragmented journey, hampering their overall experience.

Technical Gaps
In addition to design inconsistencies, technical gaps arise when Agile teams develop features in isolation from one another without a broad understanding of the entire system. Since developers focus on specific features during each sprint, integration between components can be overlooked or handled ineffectively. Features may be delivered on time, but when they are combined, compatibility and functionality issues may arise, such as inconsistent database calls, poor API integration, or usability issues stemming from inefficient back-end development.

When technical decisions are made separately without comprehensive forethought, it becomes much harder to maintain a seamless user experience. These gaps in the system create an experience that is unreliable or broken at times, which leads to user frustration and mistrust in the product.

Perhaps the biggest challenge in Agile is ensuring that design and development teams maintain a holistic perspective while addressing the minutiae of individual features. Agile teams typically have different designers, developers, and product owners working on separate elements of a product, each focusing on their tasks within specific sprints. But a product that lacks an overarching vision or design direction risks becoming a patchwork of disconnected ideas.

To counteract this, it’s vital for designers and developers to stay aligned by embracing collaborative working methods that allow everyone to regularly update and inform one another about ongoing progress. However, this can be difficult to achieve within the constraints of a fast-paced Agile environment, particularly if communication channels aren’t fully optimized or if design teams don’t have the time or resources to revisit earlier work.

The fragmented nature of Agile design can create challenges when it comes to maintaining consistency and cohesion across user experiences, from user flows to visual identity and the overarching journey. Although Agile can be efficient in driving rapid innovation and delivering features quickly, design teams must recognize the risks of disconnection, inconsistency, and fragmentation that come with such a method. Maintaining a holistic approach by focusing on collaboration, establishing a solid design system, and keeping users central to the process can help bridge these gaps and enable Agile teams to produce polished, cohesive, and user-centric products.''

The Fragility of Agile: Cultural and Linguistic Roots of UX Disintegration in Turkish Contexts

In the rapidly evolving world of software development, Agile methodology has gained significant popularity for its promise of rapid iteration, flexibility, and close collaboration. This framework champions the idea of delivering products iteratively, enabling teams to adapt quickly to user needs and changing requirements. Yet, despite its global appeal, Agile often falters when transplanted into cultural and linguistic contexts that are misaligned with its foundational principles.

Mel Conway’s seminal insight, now widely known as Conway’s Law, holds that “organizations which design systems are constrained to produce designs which are copies of the communication structures of these organizations.” His statement, originally made in 1968, continues to resonate in today’s digital product development environments — especially where Agile and UX practices are influenced by cultural and linguistic frameworks. Nowhere is this more evident than in the Turkish context, where unique cultural and linguistic patterns — such as high power distance, collectivism, uncertainty avoidance, and high-context communication — interact with Agile’s ideals, often resulting in structural fragmentation and UX disintegration.

Conway’s cultural approach reminds us that it’s not just methodology that shapes product development, but the social architecture of the teams and organizations behind it, particularly the cultural and linguistic systems through which communication occurs. This essay explores how the Turkish language, cultural codes, and communication preferences create an environment where Agile becomes fragile, and UX becomes an afterthought — not by accident, but as an inevitable expression of deeper systemic traits rooted in language and culture.

Power Distance and Hierarchical Structures in Turkish Culture
Power distance — the degree to which less powerful members of a society defer to more powerful ones — is a fundamental cultural trait in Turkey. As defined by Geert Hofstede, Turkey ranks high on the power distance index, meaning that there is a marked gap between those in leadership positions and those at lower levels. This cultural trait deeply impacts how communication flows in Agile environments, as it creates a barrier to horizontal dialogue.

In the Agile framework, one of the core principles is collaboration and feedback across all levels of the team. However, in Turkish cultural contexts, particularly in workplaces, individuals may be hesitant to speak openly, challenge decisions, or question the status quo, especially when it comes to those in positions of authority. This reluctance is not simply a product of hierarchical work structures but is also embedded in the very language used to communicate. The Turkish language, with its rich honorifics and formal structures, often reinforces social hierarchies, making it difficult for lower-level employees to voice concerns without fear of disrespect or negative repercussions.

For example, a project may focus solely on the immediate results of the current sprint and may view longer-term UX improvements as secondary. In such a context, teams may feel that they have little autonomy to address root UX issues, particularly if it requires cross-functional collaboration or challenges existing hierarchies. This power distance creates a cycle of limited upward communication, where valuable user insights or design issues remain unresolved because they are not seen as important or are filtered through cultural norms that emphasize deference.

High-Context Communication and Fragmented Collaboration
Another significant cultural trait in Turkey is high-context communication, where much of the meaning in a conversation is implied through context, non-verbal cues, and shared understandings, rather than being explicitly stated. In Agile, where clarity and direct communication are essential to team alignment and quick iteration, high-context communication can pose significant challenges.

This preference for indirect communication is particularly evident in the Turkish language, which allows for more implicit meanings and layers of interpretation. For example, a statement like “We’ll see” or “It’s a matter of time” can often convey an entire set of assumptions or intentions without explicitly addressing the issue at hand. In Agile, where feedback needs to be clear and actionable to ensure smooth iteration, this can lead to misunderstandings or fragmented decision-making.

When teams fail to communicate explicitly, Agile’s feedback loops become shallow and ineffective. The ambiguity in communication creates space for misinterpretation, and the important decisions about design or UX are often left unspoken or glossed over. This lack of direct communication, when combined with the high-context nature of Turkish language and culture, results in fragmented collaboration where key details or concerns — such as the impact of a design flaw on user experience — are either misunderstood or ignored entirely.

Collectivism and the Reluctance for Individual Ownership
Turkey is often described as a collectivist society, where group loyalty, harmony, and consensus are prioritized over individual autonomy. This cultural framework is reinforced in the Turkish language, which places a significant emphasis on group identity and relational harmony. This collectivist mindset can often stifle individual ownership of tasks, especially in Agile environments, where personal accountability for product success is a central tenet.

In such a cultural context, the avoidance of conflict and the preference for consensus often result in UX problems being overlooked or pushed aside in favor of maintaining group harmony. In the realm of design, this reluctance to challenge the status quo can hinder the ability to iterate or improve upon the product in ways that would benefit the user. For example, a designer may recognize a critical flaw in the user interface but hesitate to bring it up during a team meeting for fear of disrupting the harmony of the group. This reluctance to take ownership is culturally ingrained, with the language itself supporting deference and collective decision-making rather than encouraging individual responsibility.

When the focus is on maintaining harmony rather than resolving underlying problems, issues related to UX are often minimized or ignored altogether. As a result, Agile teams may fail to address critical issues in the user experience, leading to disjointed, inconsistent, or ineffective designs. This preference for consensus-driven decision-making undermines Agile’s iterative approach, as it prioritizes the avoidance of conflict over the pursuit of meaningful, user-centered improvements.

The Influence of Language and Cultural Norms on Agile’s Fragility
Mel Conway’s theory of systems being shaped by organizational communication structures provides a cultural lens through which to view the challenges of Agile in Turkish contexts. If products reflect the communication systems of the cultures that create them, then the flaws in Agile processes in Turkey can be traced back to the cultural and linguistic structures that govern how people communicate and collaborate.

In many Turkish environments, Agile practices are adopted as a formality, rather than being deeply integrated into the cultural fabric of the organization. While Agile rituals such as daily stand-ups or sprint reviews may be conducted, they often remain disconnected from the true, underlying dynamics of team communication. The lack of explicit, direct communication — rooted in cultural preferences for indirectness and hierarchical deference — creates a disconnect between the intent of Agile and its execution.

For example, a project owner dismiss concerns about UX flaws with a simple statement: “We don’t have the budget or time to fix this now.” This dismissal, grounded in a cultural preference for certainty and predictability, reflects a lack of responsiveness to the ongoing needs of the product. Rather than addressing the problem, the culture of avoidance takes precedence, and the issue is left unresolved. This reluctance to challenge the status quo, coupled with the high-context communication, means that UX concerns are often deprioritized and ignored.

In this way, Agile’s promise of flexibility and responsiveness becomes weakened. The communication structure, shaped by Turkish cultural and linguistic norms, inadvertently creates a system where feedback is shallow, problems are deferred, and UX is treated as secondary. This results in a fragile Agile process, one that is ill-equipped to deliver the user-centered, iterative product development that Agile is intended to foster.

Conclusion
The cultural and linguistic dynamics within Turkish society — marked by high power distance, collectivism, uncertainty avoidance, and high-context communication — present significant challenges to the successful implementation of Agile methodology. These cultural traits, deeply embedded in the Turkish language, create barriers to clear communication, ownership, and collaboration, all of which are essential to Agile’s success.

In such a cultural context, Agile becomes a brittle framework, unable to adapt and respond effectively to the needs of users or the product. Instead of promoting iterative design and constant improvement, Agile practices become ceremonial, disconnected from the underlying communication structures that shape how teams work together. As a result, UX is often neglected, and products fail to meet the needs of users, reflecting the fragmented and incomplete communication systems that dominate Turkish cultural contexts.

Conway’s Law offers a powerful lens through which to understand this dysfunction: if the systems are shaped by the communication structures of the culture that produces them, then it is clear why UX becomes disintegrated in Turkish Agile processes. The communication structure is simply not designed to foster the open, direct, and collaborative dialogue that Agile requires. Until this cultural mismatch is addressed, Agile will remain fragile, and UX will continue to be sidelined in favor of certainty, structure, and predictability.

“Any organization that designs a system (defined broadly) will produce a design whose structure is a copy of the organization’s communication structure.”

Melvin Conway, 1968

In over half a century, Conway’s Law has aged gracefully — if anything, growing sharper and more revealing as digital systems, teams, and tools have proliferated. The irony? While Agile, Lean, and DevOps have transformed how we deliver software, the communication patterns and structural incentives inside many organizations remain rigid — and invisible. And nowhere is this more evident than in how UX design is silently, steadily, and systematically compromised.

Let me explain.

Backend-First Cultures: Where Structure Becomes Product
In many product teams, especially in traditional or enterprise-driven organizations, the back-end architecture isn’t just the technical foundation; It becomes the de facto product strategy.

This isn’t just a technical constraint. It’s an organizational mirror.

Teams are formed around back-end services. Responsibilities are carved along the lines of APIs, not user journeys. Prioritization is tied to database migration timelines or legacy infrastructure needs. And inevitably, the design process — ideally user-first — is reverse-engineered to fit the backend’s existing contracts, limitations, or sequencing.

The result? Users don’t experience flows. They experience fragments.

What does this look like on the ground?
Design decisions are made after the tech stack is finalized.
Flow compromises are made to fit the structure of legacy APIs.
PMs say: “This flow is too expensive to change right now — let’s go with what we have.”
Designers are asked to “just design around the back-end.”
And all of it seems logical. After all, there are real constraints: time, budget, sprint velocity, team bandwidth.

But Conway would remind us:

“If we believe our homomorphism, then we must agree that it does. To the extent that an organization is not completely flexible in its communication structure, that organization will stamp out an image of itself in every design it produces. The larger an organization is, the less flexibility it has and the more pronounced is the phenomenon.”

Budget, Sprints, and the Myth of Incremental UX
Agile has taught teams to work small, think fast, and move iteratively. But in practice, it often becomes a license to ignore system-level UX coherence.

You’ve likely heard these lines:

“Let’s just release it and see.”
“We’ll fix UX in the next sprint.”
“There’s no time to revisit the onboarding — let’s optimize the dashboard first.”
The problem isn’t urgency. It’s orientation.

Agile, as practiced in many organizations, tends to decompose product development into atomized tickets. Those tickets reflect the team’s internal structure, not the user’s mental model. When each story is implemented to match a specific service, team, or sprint commitment, UX becomes fragmented not because of incompetence, but because of structure.

Conway again:

“Given any design team organization, there is a class of design alternatives which cannot be effectively pursued by such an organization because the necessary communication paths do not exist.”

The Anatomy of Compromise
The designer sees the disjointedness. They’ve mapped the ideal flow.
The research is clear.
The friction is known.

But when they raise the issue, they hear:

“This would require rewriting the service.”
“The front-end can’t talk to that database yet.”
“We already committed the sprint.”
And so compromise is made.
Not once, but repeatedly.
And each micro-compromise calcifies into the system’s permanent shape.

Over time:

Workarounds replace holistic thinking.
UX debt accumulates.
Design systems fragment.
Users get confused — but no one remembers how it happened.
Because it didn’t happen all at once.
It happened organizationally.

So What Does Conway See?
From Mel Conway’s perspective, this isn’t a UX problem. It’s not even a dev problem.
It’s a reflection problem.

Your product isn’t broken because of code.
It’s broken because of how your teams are wired to talk — and how that shapes what gets prioritized, compromised, and eventually shipped.

This means:

If your architecture leads design, your UX will always lag.
If your structure fragments conversation, your product will fragment too.
If no one is empowered to challenge technical-first decisions, the user will always be second.
The Systems We Deserve
When organizations repeatedly compromise UX under the name of budget, sprint planning, or technical limitation, they aren’t being lazy. They’re being consistent with their structure.

The tragedy isn’t the compromise.
The tragedy is that the compromise becomes normalized.
That “designing around the backend” becomes how things are done.
That scalable, intentional, and strategic design becomes an edge case.

But again, Conway would say: that’s just how systems work. They reflect us.

And unless we change how we think, speak, and structure ourselves — we’ll keep designing systems that work beautifully for the org chart, but poorly for the human on the other side of the screen.

A list of classic texts in behavioural science, human factors, and similar for those looking to improve their knowledge of UX.
TL/DR: A colleague said to me, “when you retire, I want your library.” Based on that comment, I decided to compile a reading list for those in UX. As I obtained my Ph.D. in Human Factors Psychology nearly 20 years ago, these are classic texts. Each entry has a brief description of why I added it to the list.
Don’t Make Me Think by S. Krug
Psychology (Design) of Everyday Things by D. Norman
Set Phasers on Stun by S. Casey & The Atomic Chef by S. Casey
Thinking Fast & Slow by D. Kahneman
Quantifying the User Experience by J. Sauro
Questionnaire Construction Manual & Annex by Babbitt & Nystrom
Cheaper by the Dozen by F. Gilbreth & E. Gilbreth Carey (with bonus film “Desk Set”)
Introduction to Human Factors Engineering by C. Wickens et al.
Improving Function Allocation for Integrated Systems Design by D. Beevis, P. Essens, & H. Schuffel
Various Standards & Guidelines
The Icon Book By W. Horton
HCI Beyond the GUI by P. Kortum
1. Don’t Make Me Think by S. Krug
I’m starting this UX reading list with Steve Krug’s “Don’t make me think: A common sense approach to web usability.” There are new editions of this book, which I have not read. The original is written in a style that exalts the principles of UX through demonstration. It is also a quick read and written for the UX professional or for management. You will gain an appreciation for the very basics of UX in the digital age.
2. Psychology (Design) of Everyday Things by D. Norman
The second entry in this UX reading list is the classic Psychology of Everyday Things, by Don Norman. Later editions of this book had a change in title to The Design of Everyday Things. I prefer the original title for two reasons. First the acronym is much more elegant P.O.E.T.. Second, I am a psychologist by training.
Don Norman also has a Ph.D. in Psychology. He also was one of the early leaders in human-computer interaction as it related to the ubiquitous GUI. I think the title change occurred around the same time as the term “design thinking” came into vogue, but I could be wrong.
Either way, Norman uses case studies to illustrate how the design of everything impacts the thoughts and actions of users. Via these case studies he introduces the concepts of affordances, gibsonian/ecological psychology, mental models, and other topics within cognitive/perceptual psychology. These case studies elucidate the point that blaming the user is a wrong strategy. Also the interested reader can apply the lessons of these cases to completely different situations. Though POET is longer than the first entry (Don’t Make Me Think — Krug), it is another quick read. Therefore a great starting point for those interested in UX as a career or wondering why it is important to the development of technology.
3. Set Phasers on Stun by S. Casey & The Atomic Chef by S. Casey
The 3rd entry has 2 books, both by Steven Casey. Both books are collections of case studies in which the design of a system led people to make errors. Like the previous entries (“Don’t Make Me Think” & “The Psychology of Everyday Things”), these books are quick reads written for a general audience. Unlike the first two, there is no analysis. The case studies lay out the facts that the human errors were the result of the system design. If I was leading a class, I would assign students to read the case studies and think (1) is it right to blame the people in the stories and (2) what steps could have been taken to prevent these errors.
4. Thinking Fast & Slow by D. Kahneman
So far, this UX reading list has focused on books for a general audience. This entry is no different. However, you may not initially see its link to UX.

Subscribe
“Thinking Fast & Slow,” by Nobel laureate Daniel Kahneman accurately summarizes what is known about human cognition. Though the book doesn’t mention UX or technology design, all of the concepts in this book have been discussed in the previous books. In “Psychology of Everyday Things,” Norman discusses Gibsonian psychology & affordances — this is the pattern recognition that defines Kahneman’s “thinking fast.” Norman’s 7 stages of action are Kahneman’s “thinking slow.” Casey’s “Set Phasers on Stun” & “The Atomic Chef” tell the stories of what happens when designs wrongly stimulate “thinking fast.” Krug’s “Don’t Make Me Think” tells how to take advantage of “thinking fast” to reduce people’s frustrations.
5. Quantifying the User Experience by J. Sauro
The four previous entries in the UX reading list have been focused on books for the general audience. This entry is written specifically for the UX researcher/practitioner. In “Quantifying the User Experience,” Jeff Sauro has created a practical guide to the topic. Most text books on the topic of research methods/ design of experiments and statistics are very theoretical and philosophical. These books have their place. “Quantifying the User Experience” describes the more practical concerns associated with these topics in very readable text. Though it is readable, it is not dumbed-down. If you are a UX practitioner who has never had a course work in psychological research methods or statistics for the social sciences, I encourage you to read this book. If you have had these courses, I encourage you to keep a copy on your desk as reference material. I find myself turning to it when I need to convince reluctant stakeholders of the best path forward.
6. Questionnaire Construction Manual & Annex by Babbitt & Nystrom
This entry goes in to more detail on quantitative UX research methods, specifically surveys. Many people believe that writing a survey is simple and it is what one chooses to do when they can think of no other way to measure/research/test a phenomenon. Neither of these are the case. A survey is a way to quantify people’s thoughts so they can be statistically analysed.
I have taught many people about survey design and administration best practices. Though there are numerous books on psychometrics that I use to develop these courses, Babbitt & Nystrom is by far the most thorough reference on the topic. If there is a question about survey design, they have done literature review and empirical research on that question.
The one limitation is that it was written in the late 1980s — so it does not address technology specific questions. Regardless, it is still my first reference when I have a question about survey design. Most notably, they did research to identify response labels for Likert-like questions that are equal intervals. This is important to ensure the data from the responses can be treated as parametric for inferential statistics.
6. Cheaper by the Dozen by F. Gilbreth & E. Gilbreth Carey (with bonus film “Desk Set”)
This entry is a bit of a diversion from the others because “Cheaper by the Dozen” is a semi-autobiographical novel written in 1950 that on its surface appears to have no connection to UX. That said it is on the list because there is a connection; I promise.
Frank Gilbreth Jr. and Ernestine Gilbreth Carey penned a loving tribute describing their lives as two of the dozen children raised by Frank and Lillian Gilbreth in the early 1900s. How does this relate to UX? Frank and Lillian Gilbreth are two of the pioneers of the integration of psychology and engineering. This integration is the heart of human factors, human computer interaction, and UX. Frank and Lillian used the principles of this nascent discipline to create a fun and efficient home life for their 11 children while being very busy working parents. Interspersed with anecdotes of children being children in the early 20th century, are a lot of great tidbits about how UX best practices came to be best practices. It is a quick and light read that I highly recommend for anyone in UX to know the founders of the discipline.
To further beat back the idea that UX is a 21st century creation, I also encourage you to set aside time to watch, “Desk Set,” which is a Spencer Tracy- Katherine Hepburn rom-com. Tracy plays a character based on someone whose work is similar to Frank Gilbraith. Hepburn plays a librarian, who fears she is being replaced by a machine. In addition to the great chemistry and acting from the pair, there are a lot of best practices of the UX discipline sprinkled throughout the story.
7. Introduction to Human Factors Engineering by C. Wickens et al.
This book is my go-to UX reference. There are 19 chapters covering the vast majority of questions related to human-system interaction from how to design research and system evaluation to design a variety of systems for usability. Furthermore, it goes into the WHY behind these best practices so that an individual knows when and how to bend the best practices. I refer to the 13 (visual) display principles frequently. The chapters on decision making, human computer interaction, workload, and safety provide a clear connection between the topics in Kahneman’s Thinking Fast and Slow (entry #4) and their application to UX. It is easily one of the most readable textbooks I have encountered. It reviews the scientific research literature accurately but clearly for non-scientists.
8. Improving Function Allocation for Integrated Systems Design by D. Beevis, P. Essens, & H. Schuffel
It is a truism, that as tasks become more automated the role of the human changes. When changing the way an outcome is achieved, we need to make sure that the changes improve the efficiency, effectiveness, and satisfaction of the human (i.e., ISO 9241–11 definition of usability). In my experience the best way to do this is the through task analysis and function allocation. These methods are very similar to the “jobs to be done theory.” The first step is to define the outcomes of performing a certain “job/task.” Task analysis and function allocation help to identify the sub-outcomes that are needed to occur to complete this “job.” Function allocation was first described by Paul Fitts in the early 1950s where the Fitts’ list described the things that humans are better at and the things that machines are better at. Though those as technology has advanced the components of each list have changed, considering what functions need to be completed and the best way of completing those is still critical to user experience.
To that end I have included “Improving Function Allocation for Integrated Systems Design by D. Beevis, P. Essens, & H. Schuffel” as the 9th entry on the UX reading list. It is a compilation of papers (written by both program managers and engineers) describing different approaches to and the benefits from function allocation. I encourage all UX professionals to become familiar with the concepts of task analysis and function allocation. This text is probably the best compilation of information on this topic.
For more information on Fitts’ List and it’s author Paul Fitts, listen to this episode of Design Critique, https://www.designcritique.net/podcasts/wordcast-paul-fitts-his-law-and-his-list/.
9. Standards & Guidelines
One of the best resources for UX professionals are the wide variety of standards that exist. These standards are based on research that has been conducted in a wide variety of contexts. It is always best to do desk (secondary) research by reviewing standards and best practices (even in other industries) before conducting primary research. Here is a list of some of my go to standards & guidelines resources:
· MIL-STD-1472G — Amongst the most comprehensive pieces of design guidance out there. It covers almost every conceivable user experience one can think of from standard GUIs to speech interfaces; from vibrations to lighting; from foot pedals to virtual reality; and excellent anthropomorphic data. The US military uses almost every conceivable piece of technology for almost every conceivable purpose (vehicles to medical; word processing & timecard software to nuclear reactors). Seriously this is ALWAYS the first place I look. http://everyspec.com/MIL-STD/MIL-STD-1400-1499/download.php?spec=MIL-STD-1472G.039997.pdf
· Usability.gov — high level software UX process and design guidance. One thing I love about this document is that it provides strength of evidence and importance for following.
· Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) — Though written for the web- they apply to most user interfaces. Also though they are labelled accessibility guidelines they are really inclusivity guidance that make your user interfaces better for everyone! http://www.w3.org/WAI/standards-guidelines/wcag/
· Section 508 — The US law for IT & communication accessibility. If WCAG doesn’t cover it and it is IT & communications related, Section 508 does. https://www.access-board.gov/guidelines-and-standards/communications-and-it/about-the-section-508-standards/section-508-standards
· NISTIR 7889 — National Institute of Standards (NIST) wrote a comprehensive list of standards for Department of Homeland Security. If you want to familiarize yourself with what UX standards exist, this is a great starting point. https://www.cpsc.gov/s3fs-public/HF-Standard-Practice-Draft-12Feb2018.pdf?CGk4Zs9GabjCnZ5RXQuSlr2toQ1aLPhJ
· Guidance on Application of Human Factors to Consumer Products — A joint effort between US & Canada’s Consumer Product agencies to define best practices for designing usable systems. https://www.cpsc.gov/s3fs-public/HF-Standard-Practice-Draft-12Feb2018.pdf?CGk4Zs9GabjCnZ5RXQuSlr2toQ1aLPhJ
· Summary of Many Relevant ISO Standards — The Human Factors and Ergonomics Society has summaries of numerous ISO standards that are pertinent to UX. https://www.hfes.org/resources/technical-standards
11. The Icon Book by W. Horton
A critical component of most user interfaces are icons. Badly designed icons can lead to poor user experience by creating features that are hidden (i.e., difficult to find) or confusion as to how to access features. Both of which can impact the usability and user experience of the technology. William Horton deftly walks the reader through when and when not to use icons and the icon design process. There are numerous examples that illustrate the principles being written about. Anyone involved in the process of system design could benefit from this book.
12. HCI Beyond the GUI by P. Kortum
The twelth item on the UX Reading List is Phil Kortum’s “HCI Beyond the GUI.” Each chapter of the book details an interface category (e.g., Haptic, Speech, etc..) and documents what best practices exist for design and testing of that interface. This book is intended for the UX practitioner or student. Like the fifth entry, Sauro’s “Quantifying the User Experience,” this book is a reference on my shelf that I turn to when I need to convince a stakeholder that it isn’t just my opinion, but research exists on the topic.

Workload, Stress, & Total System Performance
Intro: This is the 4th in a series of articles to discuss what behavioural science knows about human cognition for those in product development. The 1st article explained why product makers should understand human cognition and introduced the dual system model of cognition (aka “Thinking Fast & Slow”). The 2nd article dove deeper into one part of that dual system model, “The filter.” The 3rd article introduced Working Memory. This article dives deeper into into cognitive workload & stress and how they impact performance. Future articles will discuss leadership, motivation, among other topics. All articles will explain why this knowledge is important to making technology that has a great user experience.
What is cognitive workload?
To understand cognitive workload one first must understand the dual system model of cognition, which I described in the first 3 articles. Briefly, all information that is sensed by the human sensory systems passes through the filter, which can choose to (a) act and bypass working memory, (b) “ignore” information, or (c) pass the information on to working memory. The information passed onto working memory can be individual sensations or it can be patterns. Working memory processes these sensations and patterns to create situation awareness, which feeds into decision making, which results in action (or not) being taken on the world. In working memory there are also the processes of sensemaking (creating new patterns) and metacognition (thinking about thinking).
Press enter or click to view image in full size
a cartoon detailing the dual factor model and that automation does not change what the human cognition system does.
All processing in working memory requires what are called attention resources. Cognitive workload is defined as the number of resources required by working memory to complete a task in relation to the number of resources available. It should be noted that attention resources are theoretical constructs. As of yet, we cannot point to something physiological and say that is an attention resource. Despite this lack of knowledge as to what resources are physiologically, we know that they exist and that they are limited.
One of the best-known examples of research on the limited resources within Working Memory is George Miller’s Magical Number 7+/- 2. Essentially, Miller found that we can hold 7 chunks of information in working memory. A chunk is a pattern that has been passed on from the filter. As described in the first article, the filter groups sensations into patterns and uses selective attention to determine what patterns or sensations are sent on to working memory. This is done to ensure that the amount of information to be processed does not exceed the number of resources available. Because if the task(s) do require more resources than are available, then we are in a task overload situation and our performance degrades. When our performance degrades. So, the filter does what it can to keep us out of an overload situation.
Expertise, Resources, & Cognitive Workload
It is important to remember that the filter passes along patterns and these patterns are unique to each human, the amount of resources needed to complete a task is unique to each person. In other words the number of resources required to complete a task varies from person to person. Therefore the cognitive workload experienced in any given task varies from person to person.
One of the most studied phenomena in psychology and decision making is the differences between experts and novices on cognitive tasks. Through this research, scientists that the key distinction between experts and novices is the patterns in long term memory . This research has shown that experts’ patterns typically are more complex than those of novices (Grier, 2015). That is experts’ patterns contain more information than novices’ patterns. This means that in experts the filter passes on more information or at least the information is organized in a way that requires less resources to obtain situation awareness, which is a key input to the decision making process.
As described in the second article in this series, situation awareness is most commonly defined as “the perception of environmental elements and events with respect to time or space, the comprehension of their meaning, and the projection of their future status” (Endsley, 1988). More succinctly there are three levels of Situation Awareness: (1) perception, (2) comprehension, and (3) projection. The more situation awareness a person has, the better quality of his/her decision making.
As experts have more complex patterns than novices, they need to spend less resources to develop situation awareness. So experts experience less workload. These more complex patterns typically result in better performance with less workload for experts. That is the expert does not need to use resources to create meaning and projection as the filter has recognized the pattern and passed it on as 1 chunk to Working Memory. Malcolm Gladwell described in “Blink: The Power of Thinking Without Thinking,” they may instantaneously and unconsciously make a decision.
However, when this occurs experts rarely can describe why they came to that decision. And in fact, asking an expert to describe how they know something can lead to worse performance as they try to reconstruct the patterns in their long term memory and begin to question their decision making ability. Novices are far more capable of explaining why or how they do something than doing it. Thus supporting the adage, “those who can — do; those who cannot — teach.”
Another disadvantage of the expert is that when the pattern is passed on, it may pass along information that is not there. I am certain that everyone has written text and not realized there was a typo. This is an example of the filter passing along a pattern and not what was actually sensed. It is also how magicians do their illusions. They take advantage of the universal patterns so that we sense what they want us to sense and not what is happening.
To be clear, the cognitive workload does not correlate to the demands of the task. The same task can lead to different levels of cognitive workload depending on the people who are performing the task and their expertise. Physiological factors such as intoxication, illness/injury, hunger, and fatigue can impact the workload experienced. All of these and other non-physiological factors can cause us to experience stress, which can also impact the number of resources available.
Cognitive Workload, Stress, & Performance
Recall that the definition of workload is the number of resources required by working memory to complete a task in relation to the number of resources available. Resources are physiological; so if we are intoxicated, ill, injured, hungry, or tired, we will have fewer resources available. So even if a task requires the same number of resources on Monday and Tuesday, if on Tuesday we are tired/ill/hungry, we will experience higher workload due to fewer resources being available.
In addition, the number of resources available can change based on the experience of stress. We have heard the stories of people doing extraordinary things in moments of extreme stress. This is because in those instances additional resources (attention and physical) are made available for the fight or flight response. However, these additional attentional resources are only available for short periods of time. After which the number of resources available drops precipitously. The precipitous drop allows our bodies a chance to recharge the pool of resources available. It should be noted this recharge is necessary. If we do not recharge after extreme stress or we experience stress for an extended period of time, there will be permanent changes to the structure of the brain.

With the exception of the extreme situations, it is well documented that performance does not improve as stress increases. In 1908, the Yerkes-Dodson law established that there is an optimal level of stress where our performance increases. Part of this is due to the number of resources available, but part of it is due to task unrelated thoughts.
If the number of resources required by a task is far below the number of resources available, attention resources may be diverted away from the task to the base motivations associated with the 3 S’s (i.e., sleep, sustenance, and love). That is our mind wanders to one of these topics instead of focusing on the task at hand.

If the number of resources needed exceeds resources available, we are in an overload situation. If the number of resources needed are fewer than available, resources are diverted to thoughts of fatigue, food, & fornication. Workload, Stress, & Total System Performance
Intro: This is the 4th in a series of articles to discuss what behavioural science knows about human cognition for those in product development. The 1st article explained why product makers should understand human cognition and introduced the dual system model of cognition (aka “Thinking Fast & Slow”). The 2nd article dove deeper into one part of that dual system model, “The filter.” The 3rd article introduced Working Memory. This article dives deeper into into cognitive workload & stress and how they impact performance. Future articles will discuss leadership, motivation, among other topics. All articles will explain why this knowledge is important to making technology that has a great user experience.
What is cognitive workload?
To understand cognitive workload one first must understand the dual system model of cognition, which I described in the first 3 articles. Briefly, all information that is sensed by the human sensory systems passes through the filter, which can choose to (a) act and bypass working memory, (b) “ignore” information, or (c) pass the information on to working memory. The information passed onto working memory can be individual sensations or it can be patterns. Working memory processes these sensations and patterns to create situation awareness, which feeds into decision making, which results in action (or not) being taken on the world. In working memory there are also the processes of sensemaking (creating new patterns) and metacognition (thinking about thinking).
Press enter or click to view image in full size
a cartoon detailing the dual factor model and that automation does not change what the human cognition system does.
All processing in working memory requires what are called attention resources. Cognitive workload is defined as the number of resources required by working memory to complete a task in relation to the number of resources available. It should be noted that attention resources are theoretical constructs. As of yet, we cannot point to something physiological and say that is an attention resource. Despite this lack of knowledge as to what resources are physiologically, we know that they exist and that they are limited.
One of the best-known examples of research on the limited resources within Working Memory is George Miller’s Magical Number 7+/- 2. Essentially, Miller found that we can hold 7 chunks of information in working memory. A chunk is a pattern that has been passed on from the filter. As described in the first article, the filter groups sensations into patterns and uses selective attention to determine what patterns or sensations are sent on to working memory. This is done to ensure that the amount of information to be processed does not exceed the number of resources available. Because if the task(s) do require more resources than are available, then we are in a task overload situation and our performance degrades. When our performance degrades. So, the filter does what it can to keep us out of an overload situation.
Expertise, Resources, & Cognitive Workload
It is important to remember that the filter passes along patterns and these patterns are unique to each human, the amount of resources needed to complete a task is unique to each person. In other words the number of resources required to complete a task varies from person to person. Therefore the cognitive workload experienced in any given task varies from person to person.
One of the most studied phenomena in psychology and decision making is the differences between experts and novices on cognitive tasks. Through this research, scientists that the key distinction between experts and novices is the patterns in long term memory . This research has shown that experts’ patterns typically are more complex than those of novices (Grier, 2015). That is experts’ patterns contain more information than novices’ patterns. This means that in experts the filter passes on more information or at least the information is organized in a way that requires less resources to obtain situation awareness, which is a key input to the decision making process.
As described in the second article in this series, situation awareness is most commonly defined as “the perception of environmental elements and events with respect to time or space, the comprehension of their meaning, and the projection of their future status” (Endsley, 1988). More succinctly there are three levels of Situation Awareness: (1) perception, (2) comprehension, and (3) projection. The more situation awareness a person has, the better quality of his/her decision making.
As experts have more complex patterns than novices, they need to spend less resources to develop situation awareness. So experts experience less workload. These more complex patterns typically result in better performance with less workload for experts. That is the expert does not need to use resources to create meaning and projection as the filter has recognized the pattern and passed it on as 1 chunk to Working Memory. Malcolm Gladwell described in “Blink: The Power of Thinking Without Thinking,” they may instantaneously and unconsciously make a decision.
However, when this occurs experts rarely can describe why they came to that decision. And in fact, asking an expert to describe how they know something can lead to worse performance as they try to reconstruct the patterns in their long term memory and begin to question their decision making ability. Novices are far more capable of explaining why or how they do something than doing it. Thus supporting the adage, “those who can — do; those who cannot — teach.”
Another disadvantage of the expert is that when the pattern is passed on, it may pass along information that is not there. I am certain that everyone has written text and not realized there was a typo. This is an example of the filter passing along a pattern and not what was actually sensed. It is also how magicians do their illusions. They take advantage of the universal patterns so that we sense what they want us to sense and not what is happening.
To be clear, the cognitive workload does not correlate to the demands of the task. The same task can lead to different levels of cognitive workload depending on the people who are performing the task and their expertise. Physiological factors such as intoxication, illness/injury, hunger, and fatigue can impact the workload experienced. All of these and other non-physiological factors can cause us to experience stress, which can also impact the number of resources available.
Cognitive Workload, Stress, & Performance
Recall that the definition of workload is the number of resources required by working memory to complete a task in relation to the number of resources available. Resources are physiological; so if we are intoxicated, ill, injured, hungry, or tired, we will have fewer resources available. So even if a task requires the same number of resources on Monday and Tuesday, if on Tuesday we are tired/ill/hungry, we will experience higher workload due to fewer resources being available.
In addition, the number of resources available can change based on the experience of stress. We have heard the stories of people doing extraordinary things in moments of extreme stress. This is because in those instances additional resources (attention and physical) are made available for the fight or flight response. However, these additional attentional resources are only available for short periods of time. After which the number of resources available drops precipitously. The precipitous drop allows our bodies a chance to recharge the pool of resources available. It should be noted this recharge is necessary. If we do not recharge after extreme stress or we experience stress for an extended period of time, there will be permanent changes to the structure of the brain.

Why We Rejected the Super App Model for a Vertical Insurance Platform Redesign: Phase 1
Focus: Setting the stage, defining the problem, and the first critical decision (information architecture).
The Initial Mandate: Navigating the Strategic Crossroads:
Our project's first strategic win was validating the foundational information architecture (IA) through an immediate, targeted usability test. By decoupling architectural decisions from the aesthetic redesign, we proved that the Top-Bar navigation approach was the most efficient structure for managing a complex, multi-product ecosystem - directly supporting the corporate mandate to increase engagement and retention.
When tackling the redesign of a major mobile financial application, the first hurdle isn't visual - it's strategic. The architectural foundation determines every subsequent decision: navigation, content allocation, and long-term scalability.
Our journey began nine months ago with a clear goal - to transform a complex, legacy insurance app into a cohesive, engagement-driven platform. This is the story of how a single, high-stakes usability test validated our most crucial early decision.
I. The Challenge: A Burden of Complexity in a Reactive Sector
Strategic Shift: From Mass to PersonalizedBefore the redesign, the application reflected the typical struggles of mature financial products: high complexity and user friction. These were compounded by the inherently reactive nature of insurance - customers usually engage only after an adverse event (an accident, a claim, a health issue).
Prior to redesign - Allianz'ım AppOver time, the user experience had evolved organically, leading to fragmented information, inconsistent flows, and an outdated visual system. Key pain points were clear:
1. High Cognitive Load
Customers with multiple policies (Health, BES, Vehicle) found it difficult to locate information or complete routine tasks - especially in urgent situations. The use of a horizontal carousel to display policies forced users to swipe repeatedly to find details (e.g., from Health to Vehicle coverage). This interaction model increased cognitive load and wasted precious time, particularly in moments of crisis.
2. Limited Guidance & Cross-Sell
The application functioned mainly as a policy repository, not a financial guidance tool. The mid-section's "promiscuous" information architecture gave equal weight to self-service actions and external promotions, creating sensory overload. This lack of hierarchy obscured cross-sell opportunities and prevented the app from becoming an active engagement channel.
3. Architectural Failure & Dated UI
The Bottom Navigation Bar had reached its scalability limits, evidenced by the existence of a vague "Other" tab - an architectural catch-all for critical but non-core products. Combined with a dated UI, the experience failed to convey the professionalism and trustworthiness expected from a leading financial brand.
The Initial Mandate: Executing the Transformation
The strategic objective was to establish a modern, engagement-driven platform that would serve as the primary digital relationship channel between Allianz and its customers.
This vision aligned with a bold corporate mandate:
Transition from a traditional, agency-based model to a leading mobile insurance provider in Turkey within two years - achieving 50–60% mobile usage.
To reach this target, the platform needed to evolve through two key phases:
Phase 1: Operational Efficiency - Migrate agency functions to self-service (this phase was nearly complete).
Phase 2: Engagement & Retention - Improve usability, NPS, and satisfaction to drive retention and cross-sell.

The core transformation was therefore about shifting from documentation to activation and guidance - moving the app from a "Past/Today" model to a Future State that delivers personalized, value-driven solutions across both Lines of Business (LoB) and digital touchpoints.
Team & Timeline
The project officially launched in January 2025 as a collaboration between Ting (UX Design Partner), Product Management, and Development.
Operating in parallel tracks, our initial goal was to establish a functional navigation framework - allowing content strategy and component design to progress simultaneously.

II. Decisive Action: Choosing the Navigation Pattern
First Sketches of the Potential Navigation ApproachesBefore designing a single component, we faced a critical decision: selecting the structure that would govern the app's deep hierarchy - its Information Architecture.
The conceptual design phase narrowed the options to two models:
HUB (Super App Card Model)
Top Navigation Bar (Top-Bar)

The Great Debate: Top-Bar vs. Super App Card
Pros & Cons of Both ApproachesThe Rationale for NavBar (The Top-Bar)
We leaned toward the NavBar (Top-Bar) approach for strategic reasons:
Professional Identity & Urgency

''Design Systems vs. Design Tokens: Clearing Up the Confusion
Murat Cetin
Murat Cetin
4 min read
·
Dec 24, 2024
1






Design systems and design tokens are two essential concepts in modern product design and development, yet they are often misunderstood and conflated. This confusion leads to misplaced assumptions and, at times, an inability to recognize the inefficiencies lurking beneath the surface. In this article, I willclarify the distinction between the two, examine why the differentiation is essential, and challenge the wishful thinking that overestimates what design tokens can achieve, especially when they’re mistakenly assumed to be a design system.

What Are Design Systems?
A design system is a comprehensive framework that includes guidelines, components, patterns, and principles to ensure consistency and scalability across a product or suite of products. It’s not just about colors, typography, or UI elements — it’s about unifying design and development efforts under a single source of truth.

Think of a design system as the “blueprint” for your design and development processes. It incorporates a shared language between teams, a governance model, and documentation that guides how to use components and patterns effectively.

Key elements of a design system:

A library of reusable components
Documentation of interaction patterns
Design principles and branding guidelines
Alignment across design and development teams
What Are Design Tokens?
Design tokens, on the other hand, are the atomic units of style — the smallest pieces of reusable design decisions that can be implemented in code. They capture design attributes like colors, spacing, typography, and shadows in a way that computers and software can interpret.

For example:

PrimaryColor: #0066FF
SpacingSmall: 8px
FontSizeHeading1: 24px
Design tokens are essentially a bridge between design and code. They provide consistency in the use of values but don’t prescribe patterns, components, or behavior.

Why People Confuse Design Systems with Design Tokens
The confusion often stems from the overlapping terminologies and their relationship. Both concepts involve creating consistency across products, but their roles are fundamentally different:

Misunderstanding Scope: People assume design tokens define behavior and patterns because they contribute to UI consistency. However, tokens address “what,” while design systems define “how” and “why.”
Tech-Led Narratives: Stakeholders view design systems narrowly, seeing them only through the lens of tokens because tokens are implemented in code. This reduces the holistic vision of design systems to a purely technical artifact.
Ambiguity in Documentation: Poorly defined systems sometimes treat design tokens as the centerpiece, leading to incomplete implementation or an overestimation of what tokens alone can achieve.
Why the Differentiation Should Be Made
Without a clear distinction between the two, teams risk inefficient workflows, unmet expectations, and wasted resources.

Design Systems Focus on Holistic Thinking: A design system captures the broader context. It considers user behavior, accessibility standards, interaction patterns, and component modularity. Tokens alone cannot ensure that your card component adapts to mobile breakpoints or provides accessible contrast ratios — it’s the design system’s documentation and guidelines that ensure these qualities.
Tokens Are Just a Tool: Tokens are helpful for centralizing and enforcing design decisions, but they don’t guide behavior. While tokens define the variables, design systems dictate the relationships and rules about how those variables interact. Assuming that a library of tokens equals a complete design system is wishful thinking.
The Wishful Thinking Trap: Design Tokens as a Shortcut
The idea that having design tokens equates to having a design system is a classic example of optimism bias. This assumption fails to consider:

The need for cross-functional alignment.
The effort required for creating guidelines, patterns, and processes.
The reality that design is about user needs, not just consistent values.
Simply adopting tokens might seem like a shortcut to a unified experience, but without a robust design system to back them, they often become isolated fragments. This approach leads to:

Teams creating inconsistent patterns.
A lack of context about how and when to use certain tokens.
Design and engineering teams misunderstanding the “why” behind decision
Making Design Systems Work with Tokens
Rather than over-relying on tokens or conflating them with design systems, teams should see the two as complementary:

Start with a Strong Foundation: Begin with design principles and a clear understanding of your product’s needs. This forms the backbone of your design system.
Leverage Tokens as Enablers: Use tokens as the variables that underpin consistent styles and ensure they integrate with your system’s governance.
Document Everything: Beyond the “what,” provide documentation for the “why” and “how” — this will serve as a guide for scalable design and development.
Conclusion
Design systems and design tokens are closely connected but serve fundamentally different purposes. A design system provides a holistic framework for product consistency, scalability, and cohesion, while design tokens serve as the small, reusable variables that make a design system practical and programmable.

Misunderstanding this distinction can lead to disappointment, unnecessary effort, and subpar results. By approaching design systems and tokens with clarity, teams can foster alignment, improve workflows, and build products that truly serve their users — not just their codebase.

The Myth of B2B-fication: Why Consumer-Grade UX Fails Enterprise Complexity
For the better part of a decade, the rallying cry in enterprise software has been "Consumer-Grade UX." The premise is seductive: if we can make enterprise tools look and feel like Spotify or Airbnb, user adoption will skyrocket and training costs will vanish.
Stakeholders often come to us with a simple directive: "Make it intuitive. Make it look like the apps we use on the weekend."
But there is a fundamental flaw in this thinking. While "sleek" is desirable, the "B2C-fication" of B2B is largely a myth. It relies on a false equivalency that ignores the fundamental DNA of enterprise software. When we try to force complex financial systems, insurance back-offices, or legacy ERPs into the constrained simplicity of a social media feed, we don't just fail to solve the problem - we often break the product.
Here is why the "Consumer-Grade" approach fails when it hits the wall of Enterprise Complexity, and where the true balance lies.
The DNA Mismatch: Consumption vs. Orchestration
Consumer apps (B2C) thrive on constrained complexity. They excel at guiding a single user through a linear, high-frequency task: booking a ride, liking a photo, or ordering food. Their success lies in abstracting away the mechanics. You don't need to see the server logic to post a tweet.
Enterprise software (B2B), particularly in sectors like InsurTech or Fintech, exists in a different universe. It is built on:
Orchestrated Workflows, Not Isolated Tasks: A B2B user isn't just "filling out a form"; they are initiating a multi-stage underwriting process that triggers risk assessments, compliance checks, and cross-departmental approvals. The workflow is the product.
Data Density as a Feature: In B2C, whitespace is king. In B2B, information density is often a requirement. An underwriter assessing a risk profile doesn't want to click through ten screens of "airy" wizard steps; they need a high-density dashboard to correlate data points instantly.
The "Governance" Layer: Consumer apps assume the user owns their data. Enterprise apps must manage granular permissions (RBAC). A sleek interface that obscures who can see what isn't "clean design" - it's a security risk.

The Exception: Borrowing the Wrapper, Not the Engine
To be clear, this is not an argument for keeping enterprise software ugly or clunky. There is a "surface layer" where B2C principles are not only possible but necessary.
We can - and should - adopt consumer-grade standards for interaction patterns and feedback loops. This is where "B2C-fication" actually works:
System Feedback (Notifications): We can replace jarring, blocking browser alerts with non-blocking "Toast" notifications. This is a B2C pattern that improves the B2B experience without dumbing down the data.
Focused Context (Modals): Using clean, centered modal windows for isolated sub-tasks (like "Quick Add Policy" or "Edit Line Item") allows the user to perform an action without losing their place in the parent workflow.
Perceived Performance: Skeleton screens, smooth transitions, and optimistic UI updates are universal "delight" features that reduce cognitive load for everyone.

However, this is where the utility ends.
We can borrow the wrapper (the modal style, the notification animation), but we cannot borrow the engine.
Where B2C Logic Breaks the B2B Experience
The danger arises when we try to apply consumer Information Architecture to enterprise problems.
Imagine a Reinsurance Treaty Configuration screen. It involves 50+ inputs, conditional logic, and cross-referencing mathematical models.
The B2C Approach: Split this into a 20-step "Wizard" to make it feel simple.
The B2B Reality: You have just destroyed the power user's workflow. An expert user needs to see the inputs holistically to understand how changing Variable A affects Variable Z. By hiding the complexity behind "simple steps," you have forced them to click 20 times instead of scrolling once.

In enterprise design, clarity does not mean minimalism. It means navigable complexity.
The Product Designer's Role: Managing Complexity, Not Hiding It
As Product Designers, our job is so similar to architects; not to pretend the complexity doesn't exist. Our job is to build the Governance and Systems that make that complexity manageable.
We don't need to "Uber-ify" the insurance front-end. We need to build robust Design Systems that prioritize:
Scannability: clear hierarchies for massive datasets.
Traceability: clear audit trails for every decision made.
Efficiency: keyboard shortcuts, bulk actions, and density toggles that respect the professional's time.

The future of B2B design isn't about looking like a consumer app. It's about Enterprise-Grade Empathy: understanding that for a user processing 500 claims a day, a high-contrast, data-dense table is far more "delightful" than a beautiful, empty screen.

The "Governance" Layer: Consumer apps assume the user owns their data. Enterprise apps must manage granular permissions (RBAC). A sleek interface that obscures who can see what isn't "clean design" - it's a security risk.

The Exception: Borrowing the Wrapper, Not the Engine
To be clear, this is not an argument for keeping enterprise software ugly or clunky. There is a "surface layer" where B2C principles are not only possible but necessary.
We can - and should - adopt consumer-grade standards for interaction patterns and feedback loops. This is where "B2C-fication" actually works:
System Feedback (Notifications): We can replace jarring, blocking browser alerts with non-blocking "Toast" notifications. This is a B2C pattern that improves the B2B experience without dumbing down the data.
Focused Context (Modals): Using clean, centered modal windows for isolated sub-tasks (like "Quick Add Policy" or "Edit Line Item") allows the user to perform an action without losing their place in the parent workflow.
Perceived Performance: Skeleton screens, smooth transitions, and optimistic UI updates are universal "delight" features that reduce cognitive load for everyone.

However, this is where the utility ends.
We can borrow the wrapper (the modal style, the notification animation), but we cannot borrow the engine.
Where B2C Logic Breaks the B2B Experience
The danger arises when we try to apply consumer Information Architecture to enterprise problems.
Imagine a Reinsurance Treaty Configuration screen. It involves 50+ inputs, conditional logic, and cross-referencing mathematical models.
The B2C Approach: Split this into a 20-step "Wizard" to make it feel simple.
The B2B Reality: You have just destroyed the power user's workflow. An expert user needs to see the inputs holistically to understand how changing Variable A affects Variable Z. By hiding the complexity behind "simple steps," you have forced them to click 20 times instead of scrolling once.

In enterprise design, clarity does not mean minimalism. It means navigable complexity.
The Product Designer's Role: Managing Complexity, Not Hiding It
As Product Designers, our job is so similar to architects; not to pretend the complexity doesn't exist. Our job is to build the Governance and Systems that make that complexity manageable.
We don't need to "Uber-ify" the insurance front-end. We need to build robust Design Systems that prioritize:
Scannability: clear hierarchies for massive datasets.
Traceability: clear audit trails for every decision made.
Efficiency: keyboard shortcuts, bulk actions, and density toggles that respect the professional's time.

The future of B2B design isn't about looking like a consumer app. It's about Enterprise-Grade Empathy: understanding that for a user processing 500 claims a day, a high-contrast, data-dense table is far more "delightful" than a beautiful, empty screen.

The Fragility of Agile: Cultural and Linguistic Roots of UX Disintegration in Turkish Contexts

In the rapidly evolving world of software development, Agile methodology has gained significant popularity for its promise of rapid iteration, flexibility, and close collaboration. This framework champions the idea of delivering products iteratively, enabling teams to adapt quickly to user needs and changing requirements. Yet, despite its global appeal, Agile often falters when transplanted into cultural and linguistic contexts that are misaligned with its foundational principles.

Mel Conway’s seminal insight, now widely known as Conway’s Law, holds that “organizations which design systems are constrained to produce designs which are copies of the communication structures of these organizations.” His statement, originally made in 1968, continues to resonate in today’s digital product development environments — especially where Agile and UX practices are influenced by cultural and linguistic frameworks. Nowhere is this more evident than in the Turkish context, where unique cultural and linguistic patterns — such as high power distance, collectivism, uncertainty avoidance, and high-context communication — interact with Agile’s ideals, often resulting in structural fragmentation and UX disintegration.

Conway’s cultural approach reminds us that it’s not just methodology that shapes product development, but the social architecture of the teams and organizations behind it, particularly the cultural and linguistic systems through which communication occurs. This essay explores how the Turkish language, cultural codes, and communication preferences create an environment where Agile becomes fragile, and UX becomes an afterthought — not by accident, but as an inevitable expression of deeper systemic traits rooted in language and culture.

Power Distance and Hierarchical Structures in Turkish Culture
Power distance — the degree to which less powerful members of a society defer to more powerful ones — is a fundamental cultural trait in Turkey. As defined by Geert Hofstede, Turkey ranks high on the power distance index, meaning that there is a marked gap between those in leadership positions and those at lower levels. This cultural trait deeply impacts how communication flows in Agile environments, as it creates a barrier to horizontal dialogue.

In the Agile framework, one of the core principles is collaboration and feedback across all levels of the team. However, in Turkish cultural contexts, particularly in workplaces, individuals may be hesitant to speak openly, challenge decisions, or question the status quo, especially when it comes to those in positions of authority. This reluctance is not simply a product of hierarchical work structures but is also embedded in the very language used to communicate. The Turkish language, with its rich honorifics and formal structures, often reinforces social hierarchies, making it difficult for lower-level employees to voice concerns without fear of disrespect or negative repercussions.

For example, a project may focus solely on the immediate results of the current sprint and may view longer-term UX improvements as secondary. In such a context, teams may feel that they have little autonomy to address root UX issues, particularly if it requires cross-functional collaboration or challenges existing hierarchies. This power distance creates a cycle of limited upward communication, where valuable user insights or design issues remain unresolved because they are not seen as important or are filtered through cultural norms that emphasize deference.

High-Context Communication and Fragmented Collaboration
Another significant cultural trait in Turkey is high-context communication, where much of the meaning in a conversation is implied through context, non-verbal cues, and shared understandings, rather than being explicitly stated. In Agile, where clarity and direct communication are essential to team alignment and quick iteration, high-context communication can pose significant challenges.

This preference for indirect communication is particularly evident in the Turkish language, which allows for more implicit meanings and layers of interpretation. For example, a statement like “We’ll see” or “It’s a matter of time” can often convey an entire set of assumptions or intentions without explicitly addressing the issue at hand. In Agile, where feedback needs to be clear and actionable to ensure smooth iteration, this can lead to misunderstandings or fragmented decision-making.

When teams fail to communicate explicitly, Agile’s feedback loops become shallow and ineffective. The ambiguity in communication creates space for misinterpretation, and the important decisions about design or UX are often left unspoken or glossed over. This lack of direct communication, when combined with the high-context nature of Turkish language and culture, results in fragmented collaboration where key details or concerns — such as the impact of a design flaw on user experience — are either misunderstood or ignored entirely.

Collectivism and the Reluctance for Individual Ownership
Turkey is often described as a collectivist society, where group loyalty, harmony, and consensus are prioritized over individual autonomy. This cultural framework is reinforced in the Turkish language, which places a significant emphasis on group identity and relational harmony. This collectivist mindset can often stifle individual ownership of tasks, especially in Agile environments, where personal accountability for product success is a central tenet.

In such a cultural context, the avoidance of conflict and the preference for consensus often result in UX problems being overlooked or pushed aside in favor of maintaining group harmony. In the realm of design, this reluctance to challenge the status quo can hinder the ability to iterate or improve upon the product in ways that would benefit the user. For example, a designer may recognize a critical flaw in the user interface but hesitate to bring it up during a team meeting for fear of disrupting the harmony of the group. This reluctance to take ownership is culturally ingrained, with the language itself supporting deference and collective decision-making rather than encouraging individual responsibility.

When the focus is on maintaining harmony rather than resolving underlying problems, issues related to UX are often minimized or ignored altogether. As a result, Agile teams may fail to address critical issues in the user experience, leading to disjointed, inconsistent, or ineffective designs. This preference for consensus-driven decision-making undermines Agile’s iterative approach, as it prioritizes the avoidance of conflict over the pursuit of meaningful, user-centered improvements.

The Influence of Language and Cultural Norms on Agile’s Fragility
Mel Conway’s theory of systems being shaped by organizational communication structures provides a cultural lens through which to view the challenges of Agile in Turkish contexts. If products reflect the communication systems of the cultures that create them, then the flaws in Agile processes in Turkey can be traced back to the cultural and linguistic structures that govern how people communicate and collaborate.

In many Turkish environments, Agile practices are adopted as a formality, rather than being deeply integrated into the cultural fabric of the organization. While Agile rituals such as daily stand-ups or sprint reviews may be conducted, they often remain disconnected from the true, underlying dynamics of team communication. The lack of explicit, direct communication — rooted in cultural preferences for indirectness and hierarchical deference — creates a disconnect between the intent of Agile and its execution.

For example, a project owner dismiss concerns about UX flaws with a simple statement: “We don’t have the budget or time to fix this now.” This dismissal, grounded in a cultural preference for certainty and predictability, reflects a lack of responsiveness to the ongoing needs of the product. Rather than addressing the problem, the culture of avoidance takes precedence, and the issue is left unresolved. This reluctance to challenge the status quo, coupled with the high-context communication, means that UX concerns are often deprioritized and ignored.

In this way, Agile’s promise of flexibility and responsiveness becomes weakened. The communication structure, shaped by Turkish cultural and linguistic norms, inadvertently creates a system where feedback is shallow, problems are deferred, and UX is treated as secondary. This results in a fragile Agile process, one that is ill-equipped to deliver the user-centered, iterative product development that Agile is intended to foster.

Conclusion
The cultural and linguistic dynamics within Turkish society — marked by high power distance, collectivism, uncertainty avoidance, and high-context communication — present significant challenges to the successful implementation of Agile methodology. These cultural traits, deeply embedded in the Turkish language, create barriers to clear communication, ownership, and collaboration, all of which are essential to Agile’s success.

In such a cultural context, Agile becomes a brittle framework, unable to adapt and respond effectively to the needs of users or the product. Instead of promoting iterative design and constant improvement, Agile practices become ceremonial, disconnected from the underlying communication structures that shape how teams work together. As a result, UX is often neglected, and products fail to meet the needs of users, reflecting the fragmented and incomplete communication systems that dominate Turkish cultural contexts.

Conway’s Law offers a powerful lens through which to understand this dysfunction: if the systems are shaped by the communication structures of the culture that produces them, then it is clear why UX becomes disintegrated in Turkish Agile processes. The communication structure is simply not designed to foster the open, direct, and collaborative dialogue that Agile requires. Until this cultural mismatch is addressed, Agile will remain fragile, and UX will continue to be sidelined in favor of certainty, structure, and predictability.
