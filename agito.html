01. The December Trap: Sidelined by an Outsourced Agency

Agito powers core transactional infrastructure for 35% of Turkey’s private life, pension, and property insurance market. When I was recruited in December as the company’s first Lead Design Systems Architect, I walked into an immediate corporate paradox: leadership had already committed their year-end design budget to a third consecutive external agency.

For the first three months, I was forced into the role of a powerless spectator:

  • Deliberate Exclusion: The agency’s leadership and designers actively refused to collaborate with me. An in-house systems architect was a direct threat to their billable hours, so they bypassed design governance entirely—dealing directly with corporate account managers and Product Owners.
  • The Mobbin Echo Chamber: Without conducting a single domain analysis or researching complex actuarial workflows, the agency churned out superficial mockups copied off Mobbin. They drew whatever flattered executive vanity, completely detached from enterprise realities.
  • Blind Speculative Production: Screens were rushed through production simply to give backend developers visual shapes so they could start writing database endpoints. The mockups adhered to no established framework—neither Ant Design nor Material Design—consisting entirely of unbuildable, decorative cosmetics.

Watching this organizational drift unfold for 90 days was excruciating. I submitted an internal critical evaluation to leadership documenting that these deliverables lacked any component logic or framework feasibility. The warning was ignored. Leadership presented the agency's polished slides to the Board of Directors, and the Board enthusiastically approved them based purely on surface aesthetics.

02. The Handover of the Ruins & The Code Epiphany

Immediately following Board approval, the dynamic shifted overnight. The agency concluded its engagement, and leadership handed the entire scope to me with a naive order: "Now produce all the components first, and then build the screens."

The reckoning occurred when I sat down side-by-side with the frontend engineering team to map the agency’s approved Figma files directly to code:

Structural Dimension The Agency Figma Deliverable The Frontend Reality in Code
Component Tokens Arbitrary hex colors, bespoke padding (13px, 19px), disconnected fonts Impossible to tokenize; required writing raw custom CSS classes for every single button and table cell
Framework Feasibility Bespoke decorative widgets disconnected from Ant Design v5 Frontend had zero component matches; would require rewriting basic inputs from scratch
Responsive Dynamics Fixed 1440px desktop artboards with no responsive rules or scroll constraints Collapsed entirely on standard 1920x1080 enterprise workstations with high data volumes
Actuarial State Machines Drew happy-path cards; ignored 18-column tables, partial allocations, edge errors Failed on first real policy dataset; developers were left to guess error and loading states

The audit exposed a deeper organizational pathology: the frontend developers themselves had spent years in passive compliance—blindly writing throwaway CSS for whatever arbitrary art was thrown over the wall, without questioning architectural reusability or usability tolerances.

Looking at the audit, the lead engineer finally admitted the truth: "We cannot build this. If we try to implement these drawings across 14 products, we will spend the next three years writing throwaway CSS that breaks on every sprint."

I took full ownership of the ruins. I made an immediate executive decision: even if the resulting interface looked less flashy than the agency’s cosmetic Mobbin drawings, we would prioritize strict behavioral sets, data density, and Ant Design v5 zero-override contracts over decorative marketing fluff. We stopped drawing pictures and started building an industrial control system.

03. The Friction of Governance Under Sprint Pressure

Dismantling agency debt was only half the battle. The harder battle was the daily pressure cooker inside a monolithic engineering company.

Product Owners and management, accustomed to agency dynamics, demanded instantaneous output: "Just draw the screens fast so we can show progress to the client." They expected agency-like visual delivery speed, but without an agency team, and without understanding that drawing un-governed screens was exactly how Agito wasted the last five years.

I had to fight the "just paint the buttons" engineering mindset while operating under relentless sprint pressure. I had no luxury of taking six quiet months to design a design system in a laboratory.

“In a 250-engineer software house, you cannot stop the production line to build a pristine design system. You must build the tracks directly in front of the moving locomotive. The design system had to earn its right to exist on the sprint battlefield every single week.”

To defend the architecture against ad-hoc churn, I enforced the UX Readiness Gate. Before any screen entered Figma, Product Owners were required to lock three invariants:

  1. Operational Persona & Intent: Who sits at this screen for 8 hours (Actuary, Collector, Claims Handler) and what concrete friction is being resolved.
  2. End-to-End Task Journey: Current versus target flow mapped across module boundaries before drawing pixels.
  3. Data & Modality Boundary: Exact field counts and state transitions, determining whether the view requires a 100vh list, a 2-row drawer, or an atomic dialog.

By speaking the language of engineering contracts rather than visual aesthetics, the gate eliminated over 70% of speculative, half-baked feature requests.

The Automation Asymmetry: Rejecting Design Theater

In high-pressure enterprise environments, classical design theater collapses immediately. I had zero luxury to run 6-week discovery sprints, print empathy maps, or conduct academic persona workshops.

Worse, the organization exhibited a classic automation asymmetry: while engineering squads were equipped with cutting-edge compilers, CI/CD automation, and modern code generation, leadership still assumed design was a manual craft shop—a single person sitting at a desk, manually drawing rectangles and buttons one by one.

A database-heavy culture obstinately views design as purely cosmetic: they fail to see that design is actually the primary cost-reduction plane where testing friction, regulatory errors, and engineering waste are eliminated before a single line of backend logic is written.

Instead of accepting the manual drawing bottleneck, I turned the design function into a high-velocity industrial compiler:

  • Stabilized atomic DTCG tokens and 4 immutable floorplans as rigid mathematical contracts.
  • Leveraged an autonomous subagent compilation pipeline to assemble code-ready Ant Design v5 component recipes directly from data schemas.
  • While the company assumed I was working around the clock hand-drawing Figma frames, I was operating a software factory—delivering 40 complex enterprise screens and establishing an untouchable pattern library at speeds an entire 15-person agency could not match.

Dual-Track Pattern Extraction: Rejecting the Post-Launch Fallacy

Conventional design systems literature suggests a sequential fallacy: "Design and ship all foundational screens first, then document your pattern library."

In a 250-engineer software organization with live sprint deadlines, that luxury does not exist. Waiting for all 68 screens to finish before establishing a library guarantees 68 screens of architectural drift.

I instituted a dual-track architecture:

  • Track 1 (Live Tactical Delivery): Designing and unblocking production screens on-demand for immediate sprint commitments (e.g. currency tables, payment registers, claims queues).
  • Track 2 (Real-Time Pattern Hardening): Simultaneously extracting, tokenizing, and hardening every newly solved layout into a centralized, immutable Pattern Library.

The Self-Serve Endgame: Turning Product Owners into System Composers

A sole architect governing 14+ products cannot survive as a perpetual ticket-drawing factory. If every button addition or form parameter requires my manual Figma handcrafting, the design function becomes an organizational bottleneck.

The ultimate objective of the Pattern Library is federated self-serve design for Product Owners:

“Instead of Product Owners waiting two weeks for a designer to draw a basic parameter screen, the Pattern Library functions as a bounded Lego assembly kit. Product Owners compose standardized user stories directly using locked floorplans and pre-validated component patterns. Because the building blocks are hardcoded to Ant Design v5 and DTCG tokens, whatever the Product Owner composes is structurally impossible to misalign with engineering.”

This operational shift transforms the design role: from an exhausted reactive decorator drawing tickets, to a platform architect building the control rails that enable the entire organization to ship valid software autonomously.

DTCG Token Pipeline & Subagent Compilation

07. Retrospective: The High-Stakes Mental Model Shift

The ultimate transformation at Agito was not merely technical; it was an organizational and mental model shift.

In early career phases, designers operate within a narrow linear pipeline:

UX → UI → Design System → Screens → Handover

Navigating 14 monolithic products under intense sprint deadlines forced a fundamentally different mental model:

Business Rules → Organizational Dynamics → Technical Constraints → Delivery Economics → Governance → Decision Ownership

The core failure of the three external agencies was not that they made bad components; it was that Agito outsourced visual production without establishing decision ownership and validation mechanisms.

“A high-tier design systems architect is not paid to produce three times as many screens. They are paid to operate where the cost of architectural decisions is high: turning ambiguity into structure, preventing multi-year engineering debt, and establishing code-governed systems that an entire enterprise can operate autonomously.”
