The Dual-Track Reality

Why, in a live enterprise, a design system has to be a control system — not a style guide.

01 · FOUNDATIONS01 / 19
There Was No Quiet Room
The Rule

In enterprise, design doesn't get a research phase — it earns its foundations by running one validation step ahead of engineering, never queued behind it.

No research phase
Legacy support + new-system architecture, in parallel, across the first 3 months.
Design as a validation lane
Discover → define → alternatives → handoff, one step ahead of engineering.
Requirements from live system
Not a vacuum; decisions validated before the backlog.

Most design case studies open with a quiet research phase. I never had one. For the first three months, I was on two tracks at once — understanding the existing culture and keeping live development running, while simultaneously architecting the future strategy meant to replace it.

That sounds like a disadvantage. It wasn't. The daily work inside existing cultural constraints is exactly where the real requirements live. So I stopped treating design as a service that waits for tickets, and started running it one validation step ahead of engineering — work out the logic, define the edge cases, lay out options with honest cost trade-offs, then hand over something that has already been pressure-tested.

The Validation Clash (Vendor vs. Architecture)
The Rule

Design without technical validation isn't a product — it is a picture, and an approved picture that can't be built is simply technical debt with a stakeholder's signature on it.

I watched the debt form
Inside the company through Months 1–4, while the agency drew.
Vendor optimised for approval
Fast screens for stakeholder nods — no feasibility, no validation.
I forced validation in
Met resistance; governance slowed the approvals they sold on.

Let me correct the timeline, because it matters. I didn't inherit a finished file on day one. I was inside the company through the first four months, while the external agency was still drawing — which means I didn't find the debt, I watched it being created.

It was a clash of two methodologies. The vendor was optimising for one thing: immediate stakeholder approval. Draw a screen, get a quick nod from the business, move on — no feasibility check, no use-case validation, no system constraints. I was optimising for something else entirely: architecture that could actually be built and scaled.

So when I brought technical validation and system logic into the stakeholder reviews, there was friction — because governance slowed the approvals the vendor was selling on. They were presenting a visual fantasy; I was asking them to answer for the engineering reality. That tension wasn't personal — it was the difference between appeasement and architecture.

Inherited a Concept, Not a Conclusion
The Rule

An inherited design is an initial concept until it is proven buildable — stress-test it in the real infrastructure; never enshrine it as a final.

Imagery, not architecture
Free canvas, no grid; states & edge cases undefined.
Reclassified, not rejected
An initial concept, dropped into the real build to test it.
Evidence over opinion
A baseline you can stress-test beats a critique you can argue.

The agency's output looked finished, but it was built for presentation, not for development — free-canvas layouts, no grid, component states and edge cases undefined.

The emotional move would have been to throw it out. The pragmatic move was to use it. I relabelled it honestly — an initial concept, not a final design — and dropped it into the real build to watch what engineering did with it.

That gave me something an opinion never could: evidence. I could see, in production, exactly where the concept broke.

The Internal Reality: PO-Driven UI
The Rule

A UI shaped by momentary engineering capacity and client whim is not design — it is technical debt, drawn one screen at a time.

POs drew the screens
By hand — no benchmarks, no rules, no UX principles.
Two short-term forces
What the client asked + what engineering could build that week.
Unscalable by design
Every screen a local compromise; no shared system beneath.

The agency baseline wasn't the only thing I inherited. Running in parallel was an internal problem, and it was more systemic. When I opened the internal Figma files, I didn't find a design system — I found a visual record of engineering compromises and raw client requests.

Product Owners were drawing the screens themselves, by hand, with no benchmarks, no structural rules, no UX principles underneath. Each screen was shaped by two short-term forces: whatever the client asked for, and whatever that team's engineers could build that week. Temporary capacity and immediate whim were dictating the product architecture.

For a one-off tool you can survive that. For an ERP that has to scale, you can't — every screen becomes a local compromise with nothing shared beneath it. To scale this, we had to take the UI out of the hands of immediate whim and put it under the governance of a strict system.

01 · FOUNDATIONS05 / 19
Visuals Dictate Reality
The Rule

A mockup is not a suggestion — to an engineering team it is a build order, so governing what they see is governing what they build.

Mockups read as truth
A spec to implement — not an exploration.
The foundation's job is constraint
Define what engineers are allowed to see.
A control system
Makes the unscalable un-buildable at the source.

Here is the single most important thing I learned in my first months. Show an engineering team a mockup, and they read it the way a child reads a toy on a shelf — it exists, it is allowed, so build it. They don't see an exploration. They see a spec.

That changes everything about what a foundation is for. If visuals are read as build orders, then the foundation isn't about how things look — it is about what engineers are permitted to see, so they can't accidentally build something that won't scale.

So I stopped thinking of our design system as a style guide. It is a control system. Its job is to make the wrong thing impossible to build.

Crossing into Production
The Rule

Production is where design pays rent — hold the system to real benchmarks and prototype fast enough to see it before you defend it.

Theory → heavy iteration
Fixed deadlines; the studio gave way to production.
Held to an external bar
Serious standards, never internal habit.
Rapid prototyping
Build it fast enough to see it and react.

After the foundations, the work changed completely. This is the phase where design stops being theory and gets its hands dirty — heavy iteration, real deadlines, no room to polish forever.

I anchored it to serious external benchmarks rather than internal habit — references where every component is documented and alive, not decorative. That is the bar I am holding us to.

And I leaned on rapid prototyping to keep pace — building things fast enough to see them, react, and move. The shiny architect's office was behind me. This was the floor.

The Dual-Benchmark Strategy (Enterprise Skeleton, SaaS Muscle)
The Rule

A modern ERP borrows its structural skeleton from battle-tested enterprise frameworks and its UI/UX muscle from modern SaaS leaders — stability from the giants of the past, execution to the standard of the leaders of the future.

The skeleton — Foundations
Navigation, settings, flows → SAP Fiori, IBM Carbon, Fluent.
The muscle — Infrastructure
Stripe, Vercel, Deel, Supabase for micro-interactions & precision.
Stable underneath, modern on top
Enterprise logic, SaaS finish.

When I defined the core foundations — the main navigation, the complex settings, the heavy data flows — I refused to reinvent the wheel. I grounded that architecture in the proven logic of SAP Fiori, IBM Carbon, and Microsoft Fluent. For anything load-bearing, you borrow from the systems that have already solved it at scale.

But heavy enterprise logic is not an excuse for a dated user experience. As we moved into production and started standardising the visual baseline, I realised structural stability wasn't enough — we needed aggressive modernisation. So I shifted our benchmark compass to a second tier: Stripe, Vercel, Deel, Supabase — our true north for micro-interactions, modern patterns, and visual precision.

That is the strategy in one line: we built the skeleton using the giants of the past, and we are designing the skin using the leaders of the future.

The Architectural Pivot: Hub-and-Spoke
The Rule

Scale is only an asset while it stays navigable — 87 screens need role-based hubs, not a deeper tree.

There is one structural decision that sits above all the screen work, because it decides whether any of it is usable once there are dozens of screens.

The inherited model used a deep tree menu. That is fine at ten screens. At eighty-seven, it is a maze. So I made a deliberate pivot in the information architecture — away from the tree and onto hub-and-spoke. Role-based hubs that pull together what a given user actually needs, each opening onto a focused set of spokes.

To be precise: Fiori is still our benchmark for components and interactions. But navigation is its own strategy, and that strategy is hub-and-spoke. It is what keeps 87 screens from becoming 87 places to get lost.

The Engineering Reality Wall
The Rule

Don't spend your capital winning the argument — let the engineering reality wall settle it, and have the recovery roadmap ready for the moment it does.

The business wanted 1:1
Pixel-perfect, exactly as the agency drew.
I let reality settle it
Flagged the debt, then let the wall prove the point.
Roadmap ready
Ant Design emerged; a stall became a controlled recovery.

There is a moment every enterprise design effort hits, and how you handle it decides whether you are a service or an architect. The business wanted a 1:1 implementation of the agency's aesthetic — exactly as drawn, pixel for pixel.

I could see the technical debt that would create. I flagged it. But I didn't spend months trying to win that argument on paper. I let the organisation reach the engineering reality wall — the point where the build meets what the stack can actually do — because reality is more persuasive than any warning I could send.

When it hit that wall, the real framework revealed itself: Ant Design. And because I had seen it coming, I didn't have to scramble — I had a recovery roadmap ready to deploy. That is the moment we moved from aesthetic fantasy to a systematic production pipeline.

The Recovery Roadmap
The Rule

A recovery isn't a panic — it is a sequence: stabilise first, standardise second, govern last.

③ A-UDF
Documentation & governance published. A Governance horizon.
② Standardization
The strict, governed production standard. A Governance concern — not today.
① Recovery You are here
Components built; now applying them across the 87 screens.

So here is the roadmap I deployed — three tiers, bottom to top, and the order is deliberate. You can't govern a system you haven't standardised, and you can't standardise one you haven't stabilised.

Right now we are at the base — Tier one, Recovery. I have just finished the components, and I am moving onto the 87 screens. That is exactly where the work sits today.

Tier two, Standardization, and Tier three, the Unified Design Framework, are the climb above us — and both of those really belong to Governance, not to the present. The pyramid isn't a record of what is done; it is the sequence we are climbing, and we are at the bottom of it.

Tier 1 — Recovery: The Triage
The Rule

Don't build what you can govern — in a recovery you stabilise with out-of-the-box components and push every custom request to the back of the line.

Abandoned the 1:1 fantasy
Agency aesthetic mapped onto out-of-the-box Ant Design.
Four screen types
Overview, List, Object, Analytical — fixed templates.
Custom deferred
Every custom request to the back of the backlog.

Tier one, Recovery, was triage — and triage means you stop the bleeding before you do anything elegant. The first thing to go was the fantasy of a 1:1, pixel-perfect rebuild of the agency design. Instead, we mapped their aesthetic choices straight onto out-of-the-box Ant Design components.

Then we gave production a backbone: four core screen types — Overview, List, Object, and Analytical. Define those, and most of the system becomes disciplined repetition of known templates.

And the rule that saved the timeline: every custom-component request went to the very back of the backlog. A mature framework already gives you accessibility, interaction, and design rules for free — so you build custom only on top of it, and only once everything else is standing.

Tier 1 — Recovery: Into the Screens
The Rule

At scale, predictability beats artistry — one pipeline, one vocabulary, zero overrides, applied to every screen.

Components done → screens
Applying the system across all 87, surface by surface.
One pipeline
Research → Flow → Workbench → Handoff → Archive · ~32–46 p-days · zero overrides.
One vocabulary
Floorplans; drawers, popups, wizards by context.

With the components finished, the work now is the screens themselves — applying that recovered system across all 87, one at a time. This is the live frontier of the build.

Every screen runs the same pipeline — research, flow, workbench, handoff, archive — scoped at roughly 32 to 46 person-days, with no custom CSS to patch gaps. Every override I avoid today is an inconsistency I would be chasing tomorrow.

And underneath it all is a small, deliberate vocabulary: reusable Floorplans for layouts, and a fixed set of data-entry patterns — drawers, popups, wizards — chosen by context, never reinvented. Even the basics, like dates and amounts, came out of research, not preference.

Governance, Poured While Foundation Is Wet
The Rule

Governance isn't what you add to a finished system — it is what you build into the foundation while it is still wet, or you rebuild later.

The last system failed on governance
No rules, no buildable-gate, no decision record.
No finished platform yet
87 screens in production, foundation still being laid.
Built in parallel
Governance going in now — insurance for scale.

Let me be honest about where we are, because it matters. We do not have a finished, live platform. We have 87 screens in production and a foundation still being laid.

So I can't talk about governance as managing a system that is done — and I won't. Instead, look at why the last system collapsed. It wasn't a talent problem. It was a governance vacuum — no rules for what a component had to define, no gate between "looks done" and "is buildable," no record of why decisions were made. The moment engineering tried to scale it, it buckled.

That is exactly why I am building governance now, in parallel with the screens — not waiting for month twelve. It is insurance for scale, poured into the foundation while the foundation is still wet.

The Decision Trace Protocol
Audit Mechanism

Transforming raw PO requests into governed, zero-debt specs through 4 mandatory steps.

The Request
A product owner hands over a hand-drawn customer screen — inline forms, a bespoke layout, agency styling.
01
Diagnose the reference class. This is an Object screen, not a dashboard — so it follows the Object template, not a blank canvas.
02
Protect read-first. Inline editing breaks the pattern; the form moves into a drawer over a read-only record.
03
Reuse, don't build. Every field maps to an existing primitive. Zero custom components; the one new request goes to the back of the line.
04
Hand off at the property level. Delivered as a spec of props, tokens, and states — engineering builds from it directly.
The Outcome
A buildable screen in the system's language — consistent with the other 66, carrying no new debt.

So what does that governance actually consist of? It is becoming the Agito Unified Design Framework, and it is being built in step with the screens, not after them.

Concretely: clear rules for how a new component enters the system, how a token can change without breaking everything that depends on it, and a record of why each screen is the way it is. Floorplans carry it furthest — done right, a PM can produce a developer-ready spec without me being the bottleneck.

Designing My Own Redundancy
The Rule

A system that runs without its designer is an asset; a system that depends on its designer is a liability.

Make myself less necessary
Encode the judgment so the system carries it.
The agentic horizon
Digital-twin & AI → proactive insight, not static screens.
The through-line
Control system → governed constraints → governance that outlasts its architect.

This is the principle I would put above every other. A system that runs without its designer is an asset. A system that depends on its designer is a liability.

So my job between now and month twelve is to make myself less necessary on purpose — to encode the judgment into the system while we are still building it, so it can carry that judgment once it stands. Once that foundation is solid, the horizon opens up: an agentic layer on top of it, where our digital-twin and AI surfaces produce proactive insight instead of static screens.

The Core Conclusion

It isn't the rules. It's what they remove — decisions from the table, inconsistency across 87 screens, and a system that only works while I'm in the room.
